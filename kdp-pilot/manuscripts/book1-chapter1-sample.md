**The Beastwardens · Book One: *The Door Behind the Clock***
**Chapter One — The Thing Under the Clock Shop**

---

Bram Flint had lived above the clock shop for three years, and in all that time, no clock had ever lied to him.

That was the thing about clocks. They ticked. They tocked. They told you the truth about the time whether you wanted to hear it or not. Three hundred of them hung on the walls of Aunt Mildred's shop — big ones, small ones, one no larger than a walnut — and Bram knew the sound of every single one.

So when he heard crying in the middle of the night, he knew at once it wasn't a clock.

He sat up in bed. The house was dark. Down in the shop below, the clocks ticked on, calm as ever. But under the ticking, thin and sad, was a sound that did not belong.

Someone was crying. And they were crying *under the floor.*

Bram crept down the narrow stairs in his socks. He did not wake Aunt Mildred. She was a good aunt, but she did not believe in sounds that weren't there, and lately Bram had been hearing a great many sounds that weren't there.

In the shop, the clocks watched him with their round white faces. The crying came again — from the corner, behind the tall grandfather clock. The broken one. The one that had never worked and never sold, the whole three years Bram had lived there.

He pressed his ear to the old wood.

On the other side, something small was very, very frightened.

"Hello?" Bram whispered. "Are you stuck?"

The crying stopped.

Then a voice answered. It was smaller than a whisper — more like a thought that wasn't his own. *You can hear me?*

Bram's skin went cold and prickly. "Course I can hear you," he whispered. "You're loud."

*No one hears us,* said the voice. *Not for a hundred years.* It dropped lower, and now it sounded afraid for *him.* *You have to run. It's coming through. And when it comes, it will smell you.*

"When *what* comes—"

The grandfather clock struck.

Not midnight. Bram counted, and his heart counted with him. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

*Thirteen.*

The thirteenth bell was deep and wrong, and Bram felt it in his teeth. The clock had not worked in three years. It should not have struck at all.

And then the wall behind it was gone.

Where the old brick had been, there was a door. A small, round door of black wood, no taller than Bram himself, and it was opening. Cold air poured out around its edges — not winter cold, but something older, the cold of a place that had never once seen the sun.

Through the gap, Bram saw a forest.

It could not be a forest. It was the middle of the night, in the middle of a town, behind the wall of a clock shop. But there it was: trees taller than houses, under a sky the colour of a deep purple bruise. And between the trees, far off, something enormous moved — slow, and quiet, and far too big.

A hand closed on his shoulder and spun him around.

A woman stood in the shop. She wore a long grey coat, and the coat was soaked with rain, though it had not rained in Draybeck for a week. A scar ran through one of her eyebrows. In her hand was something that looked like a sword, except that it *hummed*, soft and high, the way a glass sings when you run a wet finger round its rim.

Behind her, all three hundred clocks had stopped at once.

"You heard the kit crying," she said. It was not a question. "Through a shut door. Through *dead iron.*" She looked at Bram the way you look at a word you have been trying to remember all day. "What's your name, boy?"

"B-Bram," he managed. "Bram Flint. I didn't do anything, I only came down because—"

"No. You didn't *do* anything." She said it almost gently. "You *are* something. Which is worse, and better, all at once."

She pushed a bundle of cloth into his arms. It was warm. It *wriggled.* Two enormous eyes blinked up at him out of a little face that seemed to be made of drifting grey smoke — a face like a fox, if a fox could be sewn from fog and firelight.

"His name's Ash," said the woman. "He chose you tonight. Which means, I'm afraid, that we don't get a choice either."

Somewhere beyond the black door, a horn sounded — one long, climbing note that made the hair stand up on Bram's arms. The woman's face went hard as the floorboards.

"That's the muster," she said. "Something's loose on the wrong side of the door, and it's a very bad night for it." She stepped backward through the doorway, into the purple forest, and held out her hand to him. The little smoke-fox pressed itself against Bram's chest and did not run.

"You picked a terrible night to be special, Bram Flint," the woman said. "Welcome to the Wardens. Now — try not to die in your first hour."

And because the only other choice was to stand alone in a shop full of stopped clocks, Bram took her hand, and stepped through the door in the wall.
