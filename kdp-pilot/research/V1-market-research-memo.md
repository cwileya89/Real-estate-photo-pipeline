## 0. How to read this memo (methodology + honesty caveats)

- **Sources are real.** Titles, authors, illustrators, publishers, series length, format, and KU availability below were confirmed against Amazon product pages, publisher sites, Goodreads, and category guides (links in §6). 
- **No invented sales figures or rankings.** Live Amazon BSR numbers and exact per-unit KU page-read earnings are not retrievable through this pipeline and change hourly, so I do **not** cite specific rank numbers or revenue. Where a title is called a "bestseller," that reflects the publisher's/retailer's own public claim, not a number I made up.
- **Price points are bands, not live scrapes.** I give the *pricing model* (trade list vs. indie/KU norm) with the band clearly labeled as typical. Treat exact cents as illustrative.
- **"Why it sells" is an explicit hypothesis**, labeled as such, per the success criteria. Each is falsifiable and reused later to justify concept choices in V2.
- **Kindle/KU weighted over print**, per instructions. The comps are tiered by *how emulatable they are for a solo KDP indie* — that tiering is the single most useful output here.

## 1. The finding that reshapes the whole pilot: LENGTH vs. CATEGORY

**The spec (8,000–12,000 words, grade 3–5 reading level, 1 illustration per chapter, ages 8–12) does not describe a "middle grade" book. It describes an *illustrated chapter book / early-middle-grade* book.** This is the highest-leverage risk in the pilot and it changes category, keywords, comps, and KU economics.

Verified length bands (§6 sources):

| Format | Typical words | Reader age | Illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early/transitional chapter book | 5,000–15,000 | 6–9 | Art on most/every page or spread |
| **This pilot's spec** | **8,000–12,000** | (declared 8–12) | 1 per chapter |
| True middle-grade novel | 30,000–60,000 | 8–12 | Sparse or none |
| Lower-MG novel | 35,000–50,000 | 8–10 | Occasional spot art |

**Implication:** a parent shopping "middle grade for my 10-year-old" expects a 150–300 page novel. An 8–12k-word illustrated book will read as thin/overpriced to that buyer and generate returns and 3-star "too short" reviews. But the *same book* positioned as an **illustrated chapter-book series for ages 7–9 / grades 2–4** is exactly on-spec, has a huge proven market (Scholastic Branches, A.M. Luzzader, Zoey & Sassafras), and is *cheaper to produce* (fewer words, and 1-image-per-chapter matches the format norm).

**Recommendation (decision needed before V2):** reposition the target as **illustrated early-middle-grade / chapter book, ages 7–10, grades 2–4**, and keep the "8–12" language only in secondary marketing copy. This is a labeling change, not a downgrade — it aligns product, buyer expectation, and cost. See §5 for the two alternatives if the owner insists on true MG.

## 2. KU economics reality check (weight-KU instruction has a catch)

KU pays authors from a shared pool **per page read (KENP)**, not per borrow. Rough public-community estimate: ~$0.004–0.0048 per KENP page. A well-formatted 10,000-word illustrated book is roughly **60–110 KENP pages**. So a *fully-read* borrow earns roughly **$0.25–$0.50**. 

- **Consequence:** short books earn little per borrow. The KU model rewards *series binge-reads and completion*, not single short volumes. Our moat is therefore **series velocity + read-through** (reader finishes Book 1, borrows 2, 3…), not any single title. This validates the series-not-standalone requirement.
- **$0.99–$2.99 sale price + 35% royalty** on a $2.99 book ≈ $1.05/sale. A sale is worth ~2–4× a full KU read. So KU is a discovery/read-through engine; **direct sales still matter for revenue**. Don't treat "KU-first" as "ignore price."
- **Cliffhanger chapter endings materially raise KENP read-through** (see Dustin Brady comp). This is a concrete, copyable tactic, already in the V4 spec ("chapter ends must pull forward").

## 3. Comparable titles (tiered by emulatability) — 15 titles

### TIER A — Indie / KU-native, directly emulatable (weighted highest)
These are what a solo KDP publisher can realistically beat. Study these hardest.

**A1. A Mermaid in Middle Grade — A.M. Luzzader** (self-pub, Knowledge Forest Press)
- Theme: underwater fantasy adventure, coming-of-age, friendship. Length: chapter-book/early-MG. Illustration: cover-forward, light interior. Series: 6 books, ages 8–12. Price: indie/KU norm ~$2.99–4.99 ebook, free on KU.
- *Hypothesis:* a **fully independent author owns a clean, evergreen niche ("mermaid" + "middle grade")**, publishes a complete multi-book arc, and lets KU read-through + a keyword-perfect title do the selling. Proof that a solo publisher can run this exact loop.

**A2. Small Flames — A.M. Luzzader** (self-pub; illustrated by Joshua Bostwick)
- Theme: young dragons competing in a tournament, each book = a different competitor overcoming a personal challenge. Illustration: illustrated chapter book. Series: multi-book. Price: indie/KU norm.
- *Hypothesis:* **"one book = one character's arc" is a template that scales content cheaply** and gives each volume a self-contained hook while building a world. Dragons are a top evergreen kid-search term.

**A3. Trapped in a Video Game — Dustin Brady** (began indie/KU; illustrated by Jesse Brady; later picked up by Andrews McMeel)
- Theme: kids sucked into video games; action/humor. Length: chapter-book/early-MG. Illustration: B&W throughout, **cliffhanger at the end of every chapter**. Series: 5 books, ages 7–12. Price: now trade; started indie.
- *Hypothesis:* **rode a high-intent evergreen search ("video game") that trade publishers under-served**, plus relentless chapter cliffhangers that maximize KU page-reads. The indie→trade path is the aspirational ceiling of this exact model.

**A4. Arthur Blackwood's Scary Stories for Kids — A.M. Luzzader** (self-pub)
- Theme: mild horror anthology-style. Series: multi-book. Price: indie/KU norm.
- *Hypothesis:* **"scary but safe" is a durable underserved kid appetite**; episodic/short-story structure lowers plotting cost per unit. Shows the same author winning a *second* niche with the same loop — i.e., the loop is repeatable (the whole point of this pilot).

**A5. Hannah Saves the World — A.M. Luzzader** (self-pub)
- Theme: kid mystery/adventure with a light social/eco angle. Series: multi-book.
- *Hypothesis:* **a recognizable "kid solves grown-up-sized problem" fantasy** + mystery's built-in page-turn engine. Third niche from one indie = template validation.

### TIER B — Small-press / illustrated-chapter-book format templates (copy the FORMAT)
Not all indie, but they define the exact page architecture our art budget can hit.

**B1. Zoey and Sassafras — Asia Citro** (The Innovation Press, small indie press; illus. Marion Lindsay)
- Theme: girl + cat solve magical-animal problems using **real science**. Illustration: art on nearly every page. Series: 10+ books, ages 6–9. Price: small-press; some KU.
- *Hypothesis:* **a clear repeatable "problem-of-the-week + STEM hook"** gives parents an educational justification to buy (parents are the buyer), and the formula makes every book fast to write. Best structural model in the set for a repeatable loop.

**B2. Dragon Masters — Tracey West** (Scholastic Branches)
- Theme: kids train dragons; light fantasy quest. Illustration: art on most pages, large type. Series: 25+ books, ages 6–8/8-10. Price: trade (~$4.99–5.99 ebook).
- *Hypothesis:* **Branches engineered the transitional format** (short chapters, big type, frequent art, numbered collectible series). The definitive template for "1 illustration cadence + short chapters."

**B3. The Last Firehawk — Katrina Charman** (Scholastic Branches; illus. Jeremy Norton)
- Theme: owl hero + magical firehawk vs. dark force; quest fantasy. Series: 12+ books, ages 6–8. Illustration: frequent.
- *Hypothesis:* **archetypal chosen-one quest scaled to 6–8-year-olds** — proves you don't need narrative complexity, you need a clear quest + likable animal hero + serial momentum.

**B4. Owl Diaries — Rebecca Elliott** (Scholastic Branches)
- Theme: school/friendship slice-of-life in diary format; full-color. Series: 17+ books, ages 5–8.
- *Hypothesis:* **diary/first-person voice + relatable school problems** convert reluctant readers; full color + short entries feel "easy," lowering the parent's perceived difficulty barrier.

**B5. Isadora Moon — Harriet Muncaster** (Oxford/Penguin; author-illustrator)
- Theme: half-fairy/half-vampire girl navigating being "different." Illustration: distinctive **pink + black** spot art throughout, author-illustrated. Series: 20+ books, ages 6–9. Price: trade.
- *Hypothesis:* **a single, instantly-recognizable two-color art identity** doubles as branding and lowers per-book art cost — a strong argument for locking a limited, distinctive palette in our style bible (V5).

**B6. The Fabled Stables — Jonathan Auxier** (Amulet/Abrams; illus. Olga Demidova)
- Theme: lonely boy tends a stable of mythical creatures on a magic island. Illustration: illustrated chapter book. Series: 4 books, ages 6–9.
- *Hypothesis:* **"caretaker of magical creatures"** gives an endlessly extensible episodic engine (new creature each book) with warmth that appeals to the parent-buyer.

### TIER C — Trade graphic-novel/cartoon hybrids (benchmark ceiling — do NOT try to match on art)
Included because they dominate the children's charts, but they win on **art volume an indie + $120 ceiling cannot reproduce.** Study them for hooks, not format.

**C1. Dog Man — Dav Pilkey** (Scholastic) — full graphic novel, hundreds of panels, ~$6–12 ebook, **not on KU**. *Hypothesis:* full-comic format + potty-humor + heart; wins on art density → non-replicable for us. Lesson: don't compete on panels.

**C2. Diary of a Wimpy Kid — Jeff Kinney** (Amulet/Abrams) — cartoon-hybrid journal, ~150+ drawings/book, ~$8 ebook, appears on KU. *Hypothesis:* first-person diary + cartoon on every page + relatable everyday humiliation. Lesson: the *diary voice* is copyable; the drawing volume is not.

**C3. The Bad Guys — Aaron Blabey** (Scholastic) — comic-hybrid, heavy art, trade priced. *Hypothesis:* villains-turn-heroes premise + cinematic comedy. Lesson: high-concept one-line premise ("bad guys try to be good") is the copyable asset.

**C4. The Last Kids on Earth — Max Brallier** (Viking) — illustrated novel hybrid (spot + full-page art), trade. *Hypothesis:* zombie-apocalypse-as-playground + video-game energy; **more text-forward than Dog Man**, so its art cadence is the *closest Tier-C book to what our budget can approach**. Worth studying as the bridge between Tier B and Tier C.

## 4. Pattern synthesis (what the data says to build)

1. **Series, complete and numbered, beats standalone.** Every durable earner above is a numbered series; KU read-through is the economic engine. (Confirms the brief.)
2. **Own an evergreen kid-search noun.** Dragons, mermaids, video games, scary stories, magical creatures — high, stable search intent that trade under-serves in the short-illustrated format. Pick ONE ownable noun for the series brand.
3. **Episodic, self-contained volumes on a repeatable template** (one character/creature/problem per book) minimize writing cost and de-risk the loop — the pilot's actual goal.
4. **Cliffhanger chapter endings** are a concrete KENP-maximizing tactic (Brady). Already in V4 spec — keep it.
5. **The parent is the buyer; give them a justification.** STEM (Zoey), "safe scares" (Blackwood), friendship/being-different (Isadora, Owl Diaries). Bake one parent-facing value into the premise.
6. **A distinctive, limited art identity** (Isadora Moon's pink+black) is branding *and* a cost control. Argues for a tight palette/style lock in V5.
7. **Don't fight on art volume.** Our conversion lever is **cover + first-chapter sample + review count**, not interior art density. Interior art is table-stakes cadence, not the differentiator. Spend the design energy on the cover (V7).

## 5. Risks, weak assumptions, operational burden (flagged per brief)

- **[HIGH] Category/length mismatch** (§1). Mislabeling as "middle grade" invites returns and bad reviews. *Mitigation:* reposition to illustrated chapter book / early-MG ages 7–10. **Alternatives if owner wants true MG:** (a) raise word count to 18–25k (more writing cost, still short for MG) and drop to sparse art; or (b) accept the mismatch and eat the review risk. Recommend the reposition.
- **[HIGH] AI-content policy + reader backlash.** KDP requires AI-content disclosure at upload (in V8 checklist). AI-illustrated kids' books face growing reviewer/librarian backlash and possible future KDP tightening. *Mitigation:* human editorial pass, disclose exactly as required, keep the pen name clean of AI branding (already in brief), never imitate a named living illustrator (in brief). **This is a live reputational risk to the imprint, not just a compliance box.**
- **[MED] KU per-borrow payout is small on short books** (§2). Profitability depends on read-through across 3 books + direct sales, not Book 1 borrows. If read-through is weak, unit economics don't clear. Day-30/Day-90 gates in the brief are the correct guardrail.
- **[MED] Discoverability cold-start.** New pen name = zero reviews. The cover-thumbnail + sample + review-count conversion triad all start empty. ARC reviews (V10) and the first-chapter sample quality (V4) are load-bearing. Amazon ads (Day-30 gate) exist because organic cold-start is hard.
- **[MED] Series lock-in cost.** Committing to a numbered series means a weak Book 1 concept poisons 2–3. This is exactly why V2 gates concept selection — get it right once.
- **[LOW] Trend chasing.** "Video game" etc. are evergreen; avoid a licensed-feeling or fad concept that dates. Pick durable nouns.
- **Operational/maintenance:** each book is ~10k words + ~12 consistent illustrations + assembly + metadata + ARC coordination. The template (V10) must make Book 2+ near-mechanical or the loop won't scale. Character-sheet + style-lock reuse (V5) is what makes art repeatable at cost.

## 6. KDP CATEGORY & KEYWORD RECOMMENDATION

### How placement actually works in 2026 (so recommendations aren't cargo-culted)
- KDP takes **3 categories at publish; email KDP support to expand to ~10** after live. Use all slots.
- Placement is **metadata-driven**: BISAC category codes + **title/subtitle** + **7 keyword fields** + **description** together decide which browse nodes and search results you appear in. Keywords and subtitle do real category work, not just search.
- **Win = rank high in a *low-competition* sub-category**, not appear in a giant one. "Bestseller in [narrow node]" badge drives the parent's click.

### Recommended categories (final pick after concept lock in V2)
Choose 2–3 **narrow, winnable** nodes over broad ones. For a fantasy-creature early-MG concept, best candidates (narrow → broad):
1. **JUVENILE FICTION › Animals › Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical** (narrow, high-intent, winnable) — *if the concept is creature-based.*
2. **JUVENILE FICTION › Humorous Stories** (winnable if voice is comedic)
3. **JUVENILE FICTION › Social Themes › Friendship** (evergreen, parent-facing)
4. Backups: **Action & Adventure › General**, **Monsters**, **Mysteries & Detective Stories**, **Fantasy & Magic** (broad — use only as one of the 10, not a primary target).
- **Avoid** competing head-on in **Fantasy & Magic (general)** — saturated by trade juggernauts.
- Set BISAC/age to **ages 7–10 / grades 2–4** per §1, unless owner overrides.

### 7 keyword slots — methodology + a working example set
Rules that come straight from the research: **long-tail multi-word phrases beat single words; don't waste a slot repeating a word already in your title, category, or author name; mine Amazon's search-bar autocomplete for real parent phrasing; each of the 7 fields can hold a multi-word phrase.**

Example 7-slot set for a *fantasy-creature early-MG* concept (**final set locks in V2 to match the chosen concept** — keywords must describe the actual book):
1. `illustrated chapter books for kids 7-10`
2. `funny fantasy series for boys and girls`
3. `dragon adventure books for kids` *(swap the noun to the concept's owned noun)*
4. `kids books with pictures ages 8-9`
5. `middle grade series on kindle unlimited`
6. `read aloud chapter book series`
7. `magic creature friendship adventure story`

Slot logic: (1) format+age, (2) genre+broad audience, (3) the **owned evergreen noun**, (4) illustrated+age reassurance for parents, (5) KU discovery intent, (6) read-aloud (parent use-case), (7) theme cluster. This mirrors the winning-comp patterns: own a noun, name the format, reassure the parent, signal KU.
